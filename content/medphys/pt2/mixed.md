## 1. Units at a glance

| Quantity | Symbol | SI unit | CGS unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Density | ρ | kg/m³ | g/cm³ |
| Force | F | newton, N = kg·m/s² | dyne = g·cm/s² |
| Pressure | P | pascal, Pa = N/m² | dyne/cm² |
| Mass flow rate | Q = ρAv (also ṁ) | kg/s | g/s |
| Volume flow rate | \(Q_V = A v\) | m³/s | cm³/s |
| Viscosity | η | pascal-second, Pa·s = N·s·m⁻² | poise (P) |
| Surface tension | γ or T | N/m | dyne/cm (1 dyne/cm = 10⁻³ N/m) |
| Wall tension (Laplace) | T | N/m | — |
| Wall stress | σ | Pa | — |
| Reynolds number | Re | no unit | no unit |
| Frequency | f | hertz, Hz = s⁻¹ | — |
| Time period | T | s | — |
| Wavelength | λ | m | — |
| Wave speed | v | m/s | — |
| Mach number | Ma | no unit | — |
| Temperature | T | kelvin, K (no degree sign) | — |
| Refractive index | n | no unit | — |
| Focal length | f | m (cm in lens problems) | — |
| Lens power | P | dioptre, D = m⁻¹ (f in metres) | — |
| Angular magnification | M | no unit | — |

Mass and volume flow rate are linked by density: **Q = ρ\(Q_V\)**. Check the unit to tell them apart: kg/s is mass flow, m³/s is volume flow.

## 2. Chapter 3 · Fluids

| Term | Definition | Unit | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laminar (streamline) flow | Smooth flow in parallel layers, fastest at the centre and slowest at the wall | — | — |
| Turbulent flow | Irregular flow with swirls and eddies in which the layers mix | — | Turbulence through a narrowed valve is heard as a heart murmur |
| Mass flow rate | Mass of fluid passing a cross-section per unit time, Q = ρAv | kg/s | — |
| Volume flow rate | Volume of fluid passing a cross-section per unit time, \(Q_V = A v\) | m³/s | — |
| Equation of continuity | For steady incompressible flow the volume flow rate is the same at every cross-section, \(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\), so a narrower section has faster flow | — | Explains the faster blood flow through a stenosis |
| Bernoulli's principle | For steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow along a streamline, P + ½ρv² + ρgh is constant, so at equal height a faster fluid has a lower pressure | Pa (each term) | Venturi mask delivers a controlled oxygen concentration |
| Viscosity | Internal friction between fluid layers that resists flow | Pa·s (SI), poise (CGS) | — |
| Poiseuille's law | The volume flow rate of a viscous fluid through a tube is \(Q_V = \pi r^4 \Delta P / (8 \eta L)\), so flow ∝ r⁴ | m³/s | Explains how a small narrowing greatly reduces blood flow; IV infusion systems |
| Laplace's law (cylinder) | The tension in a cylindrical vessel wall equals pressure × radius, T = P r | N/m | Explains why the aorta needs a thick wall and why an aneurysm risks rupture |
| Laplace's law (sphere) | For a spherical surface such as an alveolus, P = 2T / r | Pa | Explains why alveoli need surfactant to stay open |
| Wall stress | Wall tension spread through the wall thickness t, σ = P r / t | Pa | — |
| Reynolds number | The ratio of inertial to viscous forces, Re = ρvD/η, which predicts flow type: below about 2000 laminar, above about 2000 turbulent | no unit | — |
| Surface tension | Force per unit length acting along a liquid surface, γ = F / L | N/m (SI), dyne/cm (CGS) | — |
| Capillarity | The rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube, \(h = 2\gamma\cos\theta/(\rho g r)\) | m | — |
| Pulmonary surfactant | A substance lining the alveoli that lowers surface tension | — | Keeps alveoli open; its lack in premature babies causes neonatal RDS |
| TIA (transient ischemic attack) | A temporary episode of reduced blood supply to part of the brain; in this course, linked to a narrowed artery through continuity, Bernoulli and Poiseuille | — | — |

## 3. Chapter 4 · Waves, sound & temperature

| Term | Definition | Unit | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wave | A disturbance that propagates from where it was created | — | — |
| Mechanical wave | A wave that needs a medium to travel, e.g. sound | — | — |
| Electromagnetic wave | A wave that needs no medium and travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum, e.g. light, X-rays | — | — |
| Longitudinal wave | A wave in which the medium vibrates along the direction of travel, forming compressions and rarefactions, e.g. sound | — | — |
| Transverse wave | A wave in which the medium vibrates at right angles to the direction of travel, forming crests and troughs, e.g. light | — | — |
| Crest / trough | The highest / lowest point of a wave | — | — |
| Wavelength λ | The length of one cycle, e.g. crest to crest | m | — |
| Amplitude A | The height of the wave above the rest position; intensity I ∝ A² | m | — |
| Frequency f | The number of cycles per unit time | Hz = s⁻¹ | — |
| Time period T | The time for one cycle, T = 1/f | s | — |
| Wave speed v | The speed at which the disturbance travels, v = λf | m/s | — |
| Infrasound / audible / ultrasound | Sound below 20 Hz / from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz / above 20 000 Hz | Hz | Medical ultrasound uses 2–15 MHz |
| Ultrasound imaging | Non-ionizing imaging of the body with high-frequency sound; a higher frequency gives finer detail but less depth | — | Deep organs and pregnancy at 2.5–3.5 MHz; thyroid, breast and muscle at 7.5–15 MHz |
| Doppler effect | The change in observed frequency when the source and observer move towards or away from each other | Hz | Doppler ultrasound measures the speed and direction of blood flow |
| Shock wave | A cone of piled-up, high-amplitude wavefronts formed when a source moves faster than sound | — | ESWL breaks kidney stones with shock waves made outside the body |
| Mach number | The ratio of the source speed to the speed of sound, Ma = \(v_S\) / v; above 1 is supersonic | no unit | — |
| Thermal equilibrium | The state of bodies at the same temperature, with no net heat flow between them | — | — |
| Zeroth law of thermodynamics | If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body T, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other | — | The basis of how a thermometer reads body temperature |
| Temperature scales | Kelvin, Celsius and Fahrenheit: \(T_K = T_C + 273\), \(T_F = (9/5)T_C + 32\) | K, °C, °F | Body temperature 37 °C = 98.6 °F = 310 K |

## 4. Chapter 5 · Ray optics

| Term | Definition | Unit | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | An imaginary line at 90° to a surface; angles of incidence, reflection and refraction are measured from it | — | — |
| Laws of reflection | The incident ray, normal and reflected ray lie in one plane, on opposite sides of the normal, and the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, θi = θr | — | — |
| Real / virtual image | An image where rays actually meet and can form on a screen / one from which rays only appear to come | — | — |
| Focus F | The point where rays parallel to the axis meet after reflection from a concave mirror | — | — |
| Focal length f | The distance from the pole of the mirror to F; f = r/2 for a spherical mirror | m | — |
| Refractive index n | The ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in a medium, n = c/v | no unit | — |
| Refraction | The change in the speed of light as it passes from one medium into another, which usually changes its direction (not at normal incidence) | — | — |
| Snell's law | At a boundary, the product of refractive index and the sine of the angle from the normal is the same on both sides: \(n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2\) | — | — |
| Critical angle θc | For light going from a higher to a lower refractive index (\(n_1 > n_2\)), the angle of incidence, measured from the normal, at which the angle of refraction is 90°: sin θc = \(n_2 / n_1\) | degrees | — |
| Total internal reflection | Complete reflection back into the denser medium when light goes from higher to lower n at an angle of incidence greater than θc | — | Guides light along optical fibres; binoculars |
| Thin lens equation | Relates focal length to object and image distances: \(1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i\) | — | — |

## 5. Chapter 6 · Optical instruments

| Term | Definition | Unit | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lens power | The reciprocal of the focal length in metres, P = 1/f; positive for convex, negative for concave lenses | dioptre (D) | Used to state the strength of corrective lenses |
| Lenses in contact | Two thin lenses touching act as one lens whose power is the sum, P = P₁ + P₂ | D | — |
| Simple magnifier | A convex lens of short focal length; with the object inside F it gives a virtual, erect, magnified image, M = 25 cm / f | no unit (M) | Examining skin lesions, dental inspection, reading small scales |
| Compound microscope | Two convex lenses, an objective (\(f_o < 1\) cm) and an eyepiece, giving a final image that is virtual, enlarged and inverted | — | Blood cells, histopathology, microbiology |
| Accommodation | The change in the eye lens's shape, and so its power, to focus on near or far objects | — | — |
| Cornea | The transparent front surface of the eye, which does most of the refraction (power ≈ 43 D) | — | Reshaped in LASIK to correct vision |
| Retina | The layer where the image forms (real, inverted, diminished) and is turned into nerve signals | — | — |
| Near point | The closest distance at which the eye can focus clearly; about 25 cm in adults | cm | — |
| Far point | The greatest distance at which the relaxed eye can focus; infinity for a normal eye | — | — |
| Myopia | A defect in which near objects are clear and distant objects blurred, because the image forms in front of the retina | — | Corrected with a concave (minus) lens |
| Hyperopia | A defect in which distant objects are clear and near objects blurred, because the image forms behind the retina | — | Corrected with a convex (plus) lens |
| Presbyopia | Age-related loss of accommodation, so the near point moves farther away | — | Corrected with convex reading glasses |
| Astigmatism | A defect caused by irregular curvature of the cornea (or lens), giving blurred or distorted vision | — | Corrected with cylindrical lenses |
| LASER | Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation: light that is monochromatic, directional, coherent and intense | — | Bloodless-knife microsurgery, LASIK, retinal photocoagulation, dermatology |
| Laser parts | Active medium, energy source (pump) and optical (reflecting) cavity | — | — |
