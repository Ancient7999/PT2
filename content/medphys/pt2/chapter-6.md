It covers vision defects (**myopia**, **hyperopia**, **astigmatism**, and **presbyopia**) and their correction, then finishes with **lasers**—their properties, construction, and medical uses.

It begins with **combination of lenses** (the end of Chapter 5) because a compound microscope is two lenses working together.

## 2. Optics in Medicine

Optics plays a central role in health sciences. Instruments and techniques based on light are used for:

- **Microscopes** — examining cells, tissues, and microorganisms
- **Ophthalmoscopes** — examining the interior of the eye
- **Endoscopes** — viewing internal organs through small openings
- **Laser surgery** — precise cutting, coagulation, and tissue ablation
- **Medical imaging systems** — various optical and light-based diagnostic tools

These applications depend on the same principles of refraction, magnification, and focused energy covered in this chapter.

## 3. Combination of Lenses

Two thin lenses of focal lengths *f*₁ and *f*₂, separated by distance *r*, act as a single lens of focal length *f*:

```
1 / f = 1 / f₁ + 1 / f₂ − r / (f₁ f₂)
P = P₁ + P₂ − r P₁ P₂
```

If the lenses **touch** (*r* = 0):

```
1 / f = 1 / f₁ + 1 / f₂
P = P₁ + P₂
```

**Power reminder:** *P* = 1 / *f* with *f* in **metres**, unit **dioptre (D)**. Convex lenses have **positive** power; concave lenses have **negative** power.

### Worked Examples

**Two convex lenses, *f*₁ = 15 cm and *f*₂ = 25 cm, placed 5 cm apart**

```
1 / f = 1 / 15 + 1 / 25 − 5 / (15 × 25) = (25 + 15 − 5) / 375 = 35 / 375
f = 375 / 35 ≈ 10.7 cm
```

**Two lenses of power −1.75 D and +2.25 D in contact**

```
P = −1.75 + 2.25 = +0.5 D
f = 1 / 0.5 = 2 m
```

(The combination is converging.)

## 4. The Simple Magnifier (Magnifying Glass)

A **simple magnifier** is a convex lens of short focal length used to produce a magnified image of a small object.

### Principle

When a small object is placed **between the optical centre and the focus (F)** of a convex lens, a **virtual, erect (upright), and magnified** image is formed on the **same side** of the lens as the object.

### Working

The lens is held close to the eye and the object distance is adjusted until the image is formed at the **least distance of distinct vision**, N = 25 cm, from the eye.

### Medical Applications

- Examination of skin lesions
- Dental inspection
- Reading small medical scales and labels

### Angular Magnification

```
M = θ / θ₀ = 25 cm / f
```

- θ₀: angle subtended by the object at the eye when held at 25 cm (without lens)
- θ: angle subtended by the image when the object is near the focal point of the lens
- f: focal length in cm

A shorter focal length gives higher magnification.

| Object position (convex lens) | Image |
|---|---|
| Between lens and F (magnifier) | Virtual, erect, magnified, same side |
| Exactly at F | Rays emerge parallel; no image |
| Between F and 2F | Real, inverted, magnified |
| Beyond 2F | Real, inverted, diminished |

### Worked Examples

**f = 10 cm**


```
M = 25 / 10 = 2.5
```


**f = 5 cm**


```
M = 25 / 5 = 5
```


The object appears 5 times larger.

## 5. The Compound Microscope

A compound microscope achieves high magnification by combining two convex lenses.

### Components

| Part | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Objective lens** | Very short focal length (f_o < 1 cm); forms the primary magnified image |
| **Eyepiece** | Focal length f_e of a few centimetres (f_e > f_o); acts as a simple magnifier |
| **Tube length L** | Distance between the two lenses; much greater than either f_o or f_e |
| **Stage** | Holds the specimen |

### Image Formation

1. The object is placed **just outside the focal point of the objective** (between F and 2F of the objective).
2. The objective forms a **real, inverted, and large image I_1** at or just inside the focal point of the eyepiece.
3. The eyepiece, acting as a simple magnifier, produces a **virtual, enlarged image I_2** of I_1.
4. The final image remains **inverted** relative to the original object.

## 6. The Human Eye

The human eye functions like a camera: it has an adjustable lens, an iris that controls the aperture, and a retina that acts as the sensor.

### Path of Light

Light enters through the **cornea** (transparent front surface) → **aqueous humor** (clear fluid) → **pupil** (opening in the iris) → **crystalline lens** → **vitreous humor** → **retina**.

- The refractive index of the lens material is **1.437**.
- **Most refraction occurs at the cornea** (approximately 43 dioptres).
- The lens provides fine focusing adjustments (approximately 17–20 dioptres) through **accommodation**.
- Total optical power of the eye is approximately 60 D.
- The image formed on the retina is **real, inverted, and diminished**. The brain interprets it upright.

### Structure and Function

| Structure | Function | Approximate Power |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Cornea** | Major refraction | ≈ 43 D |
| **Iris** | Controls amount of light entering | — |
| **Pupil** | Opening that allows light to enter | — |
| **Crystalline lens** | Fine focusing (accommodation) | ≈ 17–20 D |
| **Retina** | Image formation; conversion to neural signals | — |
| **Optic nerve** | Transmits signals to the brain | — |

**Accommodation:** For distant vision, the ciliary muscles relax and the lens becomes thinner. For near vision, the ciliary muscles contract and the lens becomes thicker (more curved), increasing its power.

## 7. Near Point and Far Point

- **Near point:** The closest distance at which the eye can focus clearly. Average adult value is **25 cm**. At age 10 it is typically about **18 cm**. It increases with age as the lens loses flexibility. This 25 cm is the least distance of distinct vision (N) used in the magnifier formula.
- **Far point:** The greatest distance at which a relaxed eye can focus. For a normal eye this is taken as **infinity**.

## 8. Vision Defects and Their Correction

There are four common vision defects related to the eye’s lens and cornea:

1. Myopia (nearsightedness)
2. Hyperopia (farsightedness)
3. Presbyopia (old-age vision)
4. Astigmatism

### 8.1 Myopia (Nearsightedness / Short-sightedness)

- **Problem:** Near objects are clear; distant objects are blurred.
- **Cause:** The eye has excessive optical power and/or is too long. Light from distant objects focuses **in front of the retina**.
- **Correction:** A **concave (diverging) “minus-power” lens**. The diverging lens spreads the rays slightly before they enter the eye, moving the focus back onto the retina.

### 8.2 Hyperopia (Farsightedness / Long-sightedness)

- **Problem:** Distant objects are seen well; near objects are blurred. The near point is farther away than 25 cm.
- **Cause:** Insufficient optical power (increased focal length of the eye system) and/or an eye that is too short. Images of near objects form **behind the retina**.
- **Correction:** A **convex (converging) “plus-power” lens**. It adds converging power so the image falls on the retina.

### 8.3 Presbyopia (Old-Age Vision)

- **Problem:** Progressive difficulty focusing on near objects, especially after about age 40.
- **Cause:** Reduced ability of the crystalline lens to change shape (loss of accommodation). The near point moves farther away.
- **Correction:** Convex (plus) lenses for near vision (reading glasses). Bifocals or progressive lenses are often used when distance vision is also affected.

### 8.4 Astigmatism

- **Cause:** Irregular curvature of the cornea (or sometimes the lens), so different meridians have different focal lengths.
- **Symptoms:** Blurred or distorted vision, eye strain.
- **Correction:** Cylindrical lenses (or toric lenses) that compensate for the uneven curvature.

### Summary Table

| | Myopia | Hyperopia | Presbyopia | Astigmatism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sees clearly | Near objects | Far objects | Distance (usually) | Variable |
| Blurry | Far objects | Near objects | Near objects | Lines in certain directions |
| Image forms | In front of retina | Behind retina | Behind retina for near | Different foci |
| Cause | Too much power / eye too long | Too little power / eye too short | Loss of accommodation | Irregular cornea |
| Correction | Concave (minus) | Convex (plus) | Convex (plus) for near | Cylindrical |

## 9. Clinical Cases

### Case 1 — Myopia

A student can read a book clearly but has difficulty seeing writing on a distant board.

1. What is the vision defect?  
2. Where is the image formed?  
3. Which type of lens is used for correction?

**Answer:** Myopia → image forms in front of the retina → corrected by a concave (diverging) lens.

### Case 2 — Hyperopia

A young person can see distant objects clearly but has difficulty focusing on a book held close to the eyes.

1. What is the vision defect?  
2. Where is the image formed?  
3. Which type of lens is used for correction?

**Answer:** Hyperopia → image tends to form behind the retina → corrected by a convex (converging) lens.

### Case 3 — Presbyopia

An older adult gradually develops difficulty reading small print at a normal reading distance.

1. What is the most likely vision defect?  
2. What causes this problem?  
3. Which type of lens can improve near vision?

**Answer:** Presbyopia → reduced accommodation of the eye → convex (+) lenses can be used for near vision.

## 10. Lasers

**LASER** stands for **Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation**.

It produces a strong, highly monochromatic, collimated (directional), and coherent beam of light.

### Properties of Laser Light

| Property | Meaning | Ordinary Light |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| **Highly monochromatic** | Nearly one wavelength (one colour) | Mixture of many wavelengths |
| **Highly directional / collimated** | Very low divergence; narrow beam | Spreads in many directions |
| **Highly coherent** | Waves maintain a fixed phase relationship in space and time | Incoherent |
| **Highly intense / bright** | Large number of photons concentrated in a small area | Much lower intensity |

**Hazard note:** These properties make laser light more hazardous than ordinary light of the same total power. It can deposit a large amount of energy in a very small area. Eye protection is essential.

### Components of a Laser

A laser has three main parts:

1. **Active (gain) medium** — the material that produces laser light by stimulated emission (solid, liquid, gas, or semiconductor). Examples: ruby crystal (Al_2O_3 + Cr^3+), He-Ne gas, semiconductor diode.
2. **Energy source (pump)** — supplies energy to excite the atoms (flash lamp or electrical pumping).
3. **Optical cavity (reflecting cavity)** — two mirrors (one fully reflecting ≈ 100 %, one partially reflecting ≈ 95 %) that allow light to bounce back and forth, amplifying it. A small portion exits through the partially reflecting mirror as the laser beam.

**Working process:** Energy → Excitation → Amplification by multiple reflections → Coherent laser beam.

## 11. Applications of Lasers

### Medical Applications

1. **Microsurgery (“bloodless knife”)** — Precise cutting or destruction of tissue while sealing small blood vessels, minimizing bleeding and damage to surrounding areas.
2. **Dermatology** — Treatment of skin conditions and removal of unwanted or abnormal skin lesions.
3. **Ophthalmology**
   - **Retinal photocoagulation:** Laser heat coagulates blood in abnormal or leaking vessels, sealing them (e.g., in diabetic retinopathy).
   - **LASIK (Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis):** Reshapes the cornea to correct myopia and hyperopia.
4. **Medical research** — Holography for three-dimensional imaging.

### Other Applications

- Low-power semiconductor lasers in CD players, laser printers, and optical scanning systems
- Barcode scanners
- Isotope separation
- Precision measurement and communication systems

Lasers are widely used because they can deliver light energy precisely to a very small area.

## 13. Formula Sheet — Chapter 6

```
Lenses a distance r apart:
  1 / f = 1 / f₁ + 1 / f₂ − r / (f₁ f₂)
  P = P₁ + P₂ − r P₁ P₂

Lenses in contact (r = 0):
  1 / f = 1 / f₁ + 1 / f₂
  P = P₁ + P₂

Power:
  P = 1 / f     (f in metres → P in dioptres)

Simple magnifier:
  M = θ / θ₀ = 25 cm / f

Least distance of distinct vision:
  N = 25 cm
```

### Key Numbers

- Near point: **25 cm** (adult), **≈ 18 cm** (age 10); far point: **infinity**
- Microscope objective: f_o < 1 cm; eyepiece: a few cm
- Refractive index of eye lens: **1.437**
- Approximate powers: cornea ≈ 43 D, lens ≈ 17–20 D, total eye ≈ 60 D
- Magnifier examples: f = 5 cm → M = 5; f = 10 cm → M = 2.5

### Key Points to Remember

- Simple magnifier: object **inside F** → virtual, erect, magnified image.
- Compound microscope: object just **outside F_o** → objective makes real inverted large I_1; final image virtual, enlarged, still inverted.
- Image on retina is real, inverted, and diminished. Cornea performs most of the refraction.
- **Myopia:** near clear, far blurred → concave (minus) lens.  
  **Hyperopia:** far clear, near blurred → convex (plus) lens.  
  **Presbyopia:** age-related loss of accommodation → convex reading glasses.  
  **Astigmatism:** irregular cornea → cylindrical lenses.
- Laser light is monochromatic, directional, coherent, and intense. A laser consists of an active medium, an energy source (pump), and an optical cavity.

### Medical Applications Summary

- **LASIK** reshapes the cornea to correct myopia and hyperopia.
- **Photocoagulation** uses laser heat to clot blood and seal vessels.
- Laser as a **bloodless knife** cuts tissue while sealing small vessels.
- Near point moving farther with age = **presbyopia**.
